# Findings: Legal posture for Indian dentists issuing e-prescriptions, practising teledentistry, and meeting medico-legal record, consent and retention expectations under the National Dental Commission regime

Raw research output from the multi-agent sweep (October 2026). Each finding carries a verification tag:

- `gap-fill-unverified` (14): from the gap-fill round; not fact-checked

## Summary

Method note: the shared WebSearch budget was exhausted before this task started, so every finding below rests on direct fetches of primary documents (CDSCO consolidated Drugs & Cosmetics Rules PDF and 2024-2026 Gazette notifications, PCI Pharmacy Practice Regulations 2015, MoHFW Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 and EHR Standards 2016 via the NRCeS mirror, the DCI Code of Ethics 2014 and NDC Act 2023 from the NDC's own site, and nine dental judgments on indiankanoon.org). Two gaps remain: the text of GSR 220(E)/2020 (doorstep delivery) and Income-tax Rule 6F could not be retrieved from blocked hosts and are cited from secondary quotations.

E-prescriptions: the single most useful discovery is Rule 2(ee)(iv) of the D&C Rules, which defines "Registered medical practitioner" to include a person "registered or eligible for registration in the register of dentists for a State under the Dentists Act, 1948". A dentist's prescription is therefore a full-status Schedule H/H1 prescription for retail dispensing, and the Pharmacy Practice Regulations 2015 Reg 2(j) independently define a prescription as "a written or electronic direction from a Registered Medical Practitioner or other properly licensed practitioners such as Dentist". Rule 65(10) requires the prescription to be "in writing and be signed by the person giving it with his usual signature and be dated", with patient name and address, total quantity and dose. Nothing in the D&C Rules, PCI Regulations or any CDSCO notification 2023-2026 mandates a DSC/eSign; the "digitally signed" language exists only in the non-statutory EHR Standards 2016 and the (IMC-only) Telemedicine Guidelines expressly allow a "photo, scan, digital copy of a signed prescription or e-Prescription" by email or messaging. Practical position: a PDF carrying an image of the dentist's usual signature, date, state registration number and the Rule 65(10)/PCI fields is dispensable in practice; an IT-Act electronic signature (Aadhaar eSign/DSC) is the only way to make a PDF unambiguously "signed" in law and should be offered as a recommended upgrade, not a blocker. Schedule H1 is actively growing (Oseltamivir/Zanamivir 2024; Pregabalin via GSR 377(E), in force ~16 Nov 2026; draft GSR 745(E) proposes Gabapentin, Zopiclone, Carisoprodol, Flupentixol) and the dental-relevant H1 set is large (all 3rd/4th-gen cephalosporins, respiratory fluoroquinolones, tramadol, tapentadol, codeine, pentazocine, buprenorphine, alprazolam, diazepam, midazolam, nitrazepam, zolpidem, pregabalin). Schedule H2 (GSR 506(E) 22 Jun 2026) is a QR-code labelling schedule under Rule 96(6)-(7), extended to all antimicrobials from 1 Jul 2028; it imposes nothing on the prescriber.

Teledentistry and the NDC transition: no statute, NDC/EDRB regulation, state council circular or IDA position on teledentistry or video consultation exists as of Oct 2026; the Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 define an RMP as a person enrolled under the IMC Act 1956 and sit as Appendix 5 of the IMC Regulations 2002, so they do not bind dentists. The NDC Act 2023 (assent 11 Aug 2023) gives the EDRB power to regulate professional conduct "in accordance with the regulations made under this Act" (s.26(1)(b)) but the NDC's gazette list shows only the NDC Rules 2024 and an accounts rules; s.59(2) keeps the DCI Code of Ethics 2014 in force. Indian Kanoon returns zero cases since 2020 involving a dentist and WhatsApp/video/teledentistry. A video-consult feature is therefore not prohibited in India but has no safe harbour; it should be built to TPG standards (identity display, explicit consent record, digital trail, no Schedule X/NDPS, H1 only as refills for existing in-person patients).

Records and consent: consumer fora 2020-2026 consistently punish (a) no pre-operative radiograph or documented clinical baseline before RCT (Sravani Dental NCDRC 2022 / Telangana HC 2024, Rs 5 lakh; Sabka Dentist Maharashtra SCDRC Aug 2026, Rs 6.3 lakh), (b) no signed procedure-specific consent (Gaganpreet Kaur 2023; Prasenjit Das 2024 minor, Rs 3 lakh; Sravani: "Admittedly, the petitioner has not obtained written consent"), (c) absent treatment plan/progress notes ("not maintaining proper treatment record" is itself negligence under the Code of Dental Ethics: Devikar 2020, Prasenjit Das 2024), (d) prescriptions without a registration number, (e) receipts/estimates that don't match work delivered (crown material mismatch in Sravani; no line item for the bridge in Dewan 2026; unreceipted cash in Gaganpreet Kaur). Conversely, good records plus expert evidence won a complete dismissal in Jose Michael 2023, where implied consent was held sufficient for a simple extraction. The DCI Code contains the word "consent" zero times; the operative standard is Samira Kohli (real consent: nature, purpose, risks, alternatives; a relative cannot consent for a competent adult). Retention: Code Reg 3.3.1 says three years from commencement of treatment; Income-tax Rule 6F requires a Form 3C daily case register kept six years; the EHR Standards say records "must compulsorily be preserved and not destroyed during the life-time of the person, ever"; CPA 2019 s.69 is two years (condonable) and Limitation Act s.6 restarts the clock for minors at majority; dental appeals observed here ran 7-8 years from treatment to decision. Recommended default: adults 10 years from last contact, minors to age 28, then archive (inactive) rather than delete, with DPDP s.8(7) erasure honoured only where no legal-retention basis applies.

## Findings

### 1. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

A registered dentist is a 'Registered medical practitioner' for Drugs & Cosmetics Rules purposes: Rule 2(ee)(iv) defines RMP to include a person 'registered or eligible for registration in the register of dentists for a State under the Dentists Act, 1948'. Rule 65(9)-(11) therefore apply to dentists' prescriptions exactly as to physicians': Rule 65(10) requires the prescription to '(a) be in writing and be signed by the person giving it with his usual signature and be dated by him; (b) specify the name and address of the person for whose treatment it is given...; (c) indicate the total amount of the medicine to be supplied and the dose to be taken'. Rule 65(11): a Schedule H/H1/X prescription 'must not be dispensed more than once unless the prescriber has stated thereon that it may be dispensed more than once', and the seller must note their name/address and dispensing date 'above the signature of the prescriber'. Rule 65(11-A) bars substitution. Rule 65(3)(h) (inserted by GSR 588(E) 30 Aug 2013): supply of a Schedule H1 drug 'shall be recorded in a separate register at the time of the supply giving the name and address of the prescriber, the name of the patient, the name of the drug and the quantity supplied and such records shall be maintained for three years'.

**Why it matters:** Prescription PDF mandatory fields (D&C Rules): prescriber's usual signature (image or e-sign), date, patient full name AND address, each drug with total quantity and dose, and an explicit 'may be repeated N times' flag (default: single dispensing). Add the prescriber address (needed by the pharmacist's H1 register). Because the pharmacist must transcribe prescriber name+address and patient name into an H1 register, the PDF should print these in a machine-legible block; a 'repeat' field must be a deliberate opt-in. No separate register duty falls on the prescribing dentist (the register is the retailer's), but a dentist who dispenses from the clinic stock becomes a retailer and must keep it.

### 2. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

The Pharmacy Practice Regulations 2015 (PCI, Gazette 16 Jan 2015) expressly recognise electronic prescriptions from dentists: Reg 2(j) 'Prescription means a written or electronic direction from a Registered Medical Practitioner or other properly licensed practitioners such as Dentist, Veterinarian, etc. to a Pharmacist...'. The Note under 2(j) lists 'Contents of the Prescription': (i) prescriber's name, qualification, address & Regn. No.; (ii) patient name & address, age, sex, ref. no.; (iii) date; (iv) Rx symbol; (v) medication; (vi) dispensing directions; (vii) directions for patient (label); (viii) refill/special labelling; (ix) prescriber's signature and licence number. Reg 9.1(b) and 9.1(f)(i) oblige the registered pharmacist to make a 'pharmaceutical assessment' of every prescription including whether 'the prescription is legally valid', and 9.1(e)(iii) to keep 'proper documentation'. No clause addresses WhatsApp/email transmission or digital signatures, and the 2021 amendment did not add one.

**Why it matters:** This is the checklist a pharmacist is trained to apply. A dentist's PDF should carry every item in the PCI Note (notably age, sex, qualification and a reference/prescription number) so a chemist has no basis to refuse it as 'not legally valid'. Because the pharmacist bears a documentation duty, embed a QR code/short URL that lets the chemist verify the prescription (status, prescriber registration) and log the dispensing: that turns the app's prescription into the most verifiable thing on the counter and is a genuine differentiator for a dental-only product.

### 3. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

A digital signature on an e-prescription is recommended, not legally mandatory, for a dentist. The only 'digitally signed' requirement is in the MoHFW EHR Standards for India 2016 (a voluntary adoption standard, not subordinate legislation): 'For the purpose of e-Prescription, implementers must ensure that the electronic version is digitally signed by a registered medical practitioner, and its non-repudiation is ensured at all times. The pharmacists shall be able to print a copy of e-Prescription in the required format along with other relevant digital authentication details' and 'The printed prescription will need to be in the PCI prescribed format'. The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 (3.6.4.2) instead accept that an 'RMP shall provide photo, scan, digital copy of a signed prescription or e-Prescription to the patient via email or any messaging platform', and require explicit patient consent if the RMP transmits the prescription directly to a pharmacy. The ABDM/NRCeS FHIR PrescriptionRecord profile makes Composition.author (Practitioner) mandatory but contains no mandatory Bundle.signature or digital-signature element. No CDSCO/DCGI notification 2023-2026 addresses e-prescriptions or online sale; the e-pharmacy draft GSR 817(E) of 28 Aug 2018 was never finalised, and the COVID-era doorstep-delivery notification GSR 220(E) of 26 Mar 2020 (listed by CDSCO as 'Doorstep delivery of the drugs by retail - Section 26B') is the only instrument that ever expressly contemplated dispensing on an emailed prescription.

**Why it matters:** Ship v1 with (a) an image of the dentist's wet 'usual signature' captured once and applied to every PDF plus (b) a server-side tamper-evident hash and verification URL/QR; offer Aadhaar eSign/DSC (IT Act s.3/3A electronic signature) as an optional 'ABDM-grade' setting rather than a gate. Do not advertise prescriptions as 'digitally signed' unless eSign is actually used. Build the PDF to the PCI format so it also satisfies the EHR Standards' print requirement. Treat GSR 220(E) as lapsed/COVID-specific; do not rely on it for home-delivery claims.

### 4. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

Schedule H1 (GSR 588(E), 30 Aug 2013, 46 drugs) has grown to serial 51+ and the dentally relevant set is large. Original list includes: Cefdinir, Cefditoren, Cefepime, Cefetamet, Cefixime, Cefoperazone, Cefotaxime, Cefpirome, Cefpodoxime, Ceftazidime, Ceftibuten, Ceftizoxime, Ceftriaxone; Levofloxacin, Moxifloxacin, Gemifloxacin, Sparfloxacin, Prulifloxacin, Balofloxacin; Tramadol, Codeine, Pentazocine, Buprenorphine; Alprazolam, Diazepam, Midazolam, Nitrazepam, Chlordiazepoxide, Zolpidem; plus carbapenems and anti-TB drugs. Additions: Oxytocin (serial 47, GSR 794/795(E) 21 Aug 2018), Tapentadol (serial 48, GSR 258(E) 7 Apr 2021), Oseltamivir and Zanamivir (49-50, GSR 95(E) 5 Feb 2024), Pregabalin (serial 51, GSR 377(E) 13 May 2026, published 20 May 2026, 'shall come into force after one eighty days from the date of publication', i.e. ~16 Nov 2026). H1 packs must bear 'SCHEDULE H1 DRUG - WARNING... Not to be sold by retail without the prescription of a Registered Medical Practitioner' (Rule 97(1)(e)). Common dental drugs NOT in H1 (amoxicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, metronidazole, clindamycin, azithromycin, NSAIDs, chlorhexidine) remain ordinary Schedule H.

**Why it matters:** The drug master must carry a schedule flag (H / H1 / X / NDPS) with an effective-date history, because H1 status is time-dependent (pregabalin flips in Nov 2026; gabapentin likely 2027). H1 items should trigger: no-repeat default, mandatory patient address and prescriber address on the PDF, a 'pharmacist register' notice, and a block on tele-issued first prescriptions. Keep a CDSCO notifications watcher; the list changed three times in 2024-2026.

### 5. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

Schedule H2 does not affect dental prescribing; it is a manufacturer labelling (QR/bar-code) schedule referenced by Rule 96(6)-(7). GSR 506(E) of 22 Jun 2026 (Drugs (Seventh Amendment) Rules 2026, finalising draft GSR 757(E) of 16 Oct 2025) renumbers the existing list as 'Table 1' and adds 'Table 2': 1. All Vaccines; 2. All Antimicrobials; 3. All Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances under the NDPS Act 1985; 4. All Anticancer drugs. It 'shall come into force on the 1st day of July, 2027, except serial number 2 in Table 2 [All Antimicrobials], which shall come into force on the 1st day of July, 2028'.

**Why it matters:** No prescriber-side obligation, but from July 2028 every antibiotic pack a dental clinic stocks will carry a scannable code with product/batch data. Plan an inventory/dispensing scan feature that reads the Schedule H2 code to auto-fill batch, expiry and manufacturer in the clinic's dispensing record; that converts a compliance rule into a workflow win for clinics that dispense in-house.

### 6. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

The Telemedicine Practice Guidelines 2020 do not bind dentists and nothing else fills the gap. TPG 1.1.3: 'a Registered Medical Practitioner is defined as a person who is enrolled in the State Medical Register or the Indian Medical Register under the IMC Act 1956'; the document 'constitutes Appendix 5 of the Indian Medical Council (Professional Conduct, Etiquette and Ethics) Regulations, 2002'. As of Oct 2026: the NDC gazette page lists only the NDC Act 2023, NDC Rules 2024 and NDC (Annual Statement of Accounts...) Rules 2024 - no conduct, ethics, registration, advertising or teledentistry regulation, draft or final; the NDC homepage 'What's New' items (Sep-Oct 2026) concern NEET schedules, admissions, anti-ragging, temporary registration and a workforce study; the IDA website has no teledentistry/tele-consultation/e-prescription statement; Indian Kanoon returns 'No matching results' for dentist AND (WhatsApp OR 'video consultation' OR teledentistry OR 'Telemedicine Practice Guidelines') from 1 Jan 2020. The TPG's own preamble notes the 2018 Bombay High Court judgment (Deepa Pawaskar) 'had created uncertainty about the place and legitimacy of telemedicine because an appropriate framework does not exist' - that uncertainty still applies to dentists.

**Why it matters:** A video-consult feature can ship in India (no prohibition), but it carries no regulatory safe harbour for dentists. Mitigate by voluntarily implementing the TPG controls as product defaults: display dentist name, qualification and registration number in the consult UI and on any e-communication/receipt (TPG 3.2.5); record explicit consent for the tele-consult (TPG 3.4.3 allows 'email, text or audio/video'); keep a log of every interaction (TPG 3.7.2.1-3); allow only List-O-type and refill prescriptions, block Schedule X/NDPS entirely, and block Schedule H1 first-prescriptions by video. Label the feature 'tele-triage / follow-up' rather than 'online treatment' in India until the EDRB issues regulations.

### 8. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

The DCI (Revised Dentists Code of Ethics) Regulations 2014 (Gazette 27 Jun 2014) fix the dentist-specific record, prescription and estimate duties and contain no consent clause at all (the word 'consent' does not appear). Reg 3.3.1: records 'must be preserved for a minimum period of three years from the date of commencement of the treatment in a format determined by the Council or accepted as a standard mode of documentation'; 3.3.2: on request by patient/attendant/legal authority, records 'may be issued... within 72 hours after having obtained a valid receipt'; 3.3.3: a Register of Medical Certificates with patient signature/thumb mark, address and an identification mark; 3.3.4: 'Efforts shall be made to digitalize dental/medical records'. 3.4.1: 'Every Dental practitioner shall display the registration number accorded to him by the State Dental Council in his clinic and in all his prescriptions, certificates and money receipts'. 3.5: prescribe 'as far as possible... in a generic form'. 5.7.1: 'clearly indicate the cost of treatment for the procedure and make an estimate of all costs likely to be incurred. Any increase in subsequent cost should be justified... Prescription should also make it clear if the Dental Surgeon himself dispensed any medicine'. 5.7.2: 'write his name and designation in full along with the recognized dental degrees and the registration particulars in his prescription letter head'. The only amendment since (draft 2nd Amendment, 5 Sep 2018) adds a CDE non-participation misconduct clause. The IDA Code of Ethics copies these verbatim (4.3, 4.4) and adds 5.1.5 'accurate and comprehensive records of medical and dental histories, clinical findings, diagnoses, treatment plans, and treatments', a duty to release copies to the patient or their nominee, and a prohibition on falsifying 'written or electronic documents including patient records'.

**Why it matters:** Mandatory prescription-PDF fields from the Code: dentist's full name, designation, recognised degrees, State Dental Council registration number (letterhead), generic name alongside brand, and a 'dispensed in-clinic' marker per line item. Mandatory receipt field: registration number. Mandatory estimate object: itemised pre-treatment estimate with a versioned 'increase justification' note. Build a 'records request' workflow with a 72-hour SLA, receipt capture and an export log; and a medical-certificate register with ID-mark and signature capture. Since the Code is silent on consent, the consent module's standard comes from case law (next finding), not regulation.

### 9. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

Consent standard applied to dentists: Samira Kohli v. Prabha Manchanda (2008) 2 SCC 1 requires 'real and valid' consent based on adequate information about nature, purpose, expected effects, alternatives and substantial risks; consent for one procedure does not extend to another; and 'when a patient is a competent adult, there is no question of someone else giving consent on her behalf' (the doctor was denied her entire fee plus Rs 25,000). Dental applications 2022-2026: Sravani Dental Hospital v. Anitha Tangellamudi (NCDRC, 8 Mar 2022, Rs 5 lakh + Rs 10,000, 6% interest; Telangana HC WP 25784/2022 dismissed 25 Jan 2024) - the patient 'was not told that RCT would make her teeth non-vital', experts 'were not aware of the patient-doctor conversation on informed consent', and the HC recorded 'Admittedly, the petitioner has not obtained written consent'. Dr. Gaganpreet Kaur v. Gurmeet Kaur (Punjab SCDRC, 24 Nov 2023): 'no consent form was ever signed by the patient', risks of RCT not proved to be explained, the IDA protocol on informed consent for endodontic therapy was used as the yardstick. Dr. Prasenjit Das v. Aditi Sarkar (minor) (WB SCDRC, 24 Jun 2024, Rs 3 lakh upheld): no written consent from the father before orthodontic extraction/alignment was itself held negligent. Conversely Dr. Jose Michael v. Auseppachan (Kerala SCDRC, 18 Apr 2023): for a routine third-molar extraction 'written consent is not mandatory' and implied consent by sitting in the chair sufficed; the complaint was dismissed on the strength of clinic records and two experts. Narinder Singh v. Harpreet Eye and Dental Care (Punjab SCDRC, 20 Feb 2024, ~Rs 9.6 lakh): a signed 'Dental Implant Patient Information and Consent Form' did not protect the dentist where implants perforated the mental nerve and the complainant alleged no record of injections/medicines was supplied.

**Why it matters:** Consent record legal minimum (mandatory): procedure-specific form (RCT, extraction, implant, ortho, crown/prosthesis, sedation) naming the tooth numbers, stating nature/purpose, material risks in plain language (e.g. RCT = non-vital tooth; implant = nerve injury/paresthesia; extraction = dry socket, fracture), alternatives including no treatment, cost estimate reference, the signer's identity and relationship (self; parent/guardian for minors with the minor's assent noted), dentist's name/registration number, date-time, and the patient's signature or OTP/biometric e-sign with an immutable audit trail. Recommended: a free-text 'discussion note' field (what was explained, questions asked) captured by the dentist, because fora look for evidence of the conversation, not just a signature; a separate 'treatment-plan change' re-consent trigger; and 'implied consent' templates for examination/scaling/simple restorations so clinics are not forced to over-document trivial care.

### 10. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

What consumer fora expect to see in a dental record (and punish when absent), from 2020-2026 judgments: (1) pre-operative radiograph before any pulp therapy - Sabka Dentist v. Kalpana Tripathi (Maharashtra SCDRC, 7 Aug 2026; Rs 4,32,208 @12% + Rs 2 lakh): clinic 'failed to produce verified pre-operative intraoral periapical (IOPA) radiographs', had no 'documented clinical baselines' for nine RCTs, and the Commission held 'baseline diagnostic imaging is mandatory before devitalizing healthy pulpal tissue'; Sravani (NCDRC 2022) and Prasenjit Das (2024, 'gross negligence' where no X-ray was recorded and the prescription did not mention one). (2) A written treatment plan and progress notes - Sangeeta Devikar v. Ashutosh Pathak (Maharashtra SCDRC, 18 Aug 2020, Rs 2 lakh + Rs 10,000): file held only receipts and handwritten notes, 'no treatment plan, progress details, or signed informed consent'; 'not maintaining proper treatment record' is negligence under the Code of Dental Ethics; Prasenjit Das repeated this verbatim. (3) Registration number on the prescription (absent in Prasenjit Das; held against the dentist). (4) Receipts and itemised bills matching what was promised - Sravani: 'metal-ceramic crowns were supplied instead of the metal-free ceramic promised and billed'; Dr. Sanjeev Dewan v. Hotti (SCDRC, 28 Sep 2026): 'no specific mention of any separate charges relating to the dental bridge', so the whole Rs 64,000 implant fee was refunded; Gaganpreet Kaur: 'There is no receipt/supporting document with regard to any such payment' for Rs 90,000 cash. (5) Patient-specific warranty/lab records (Sabka Dentist: warranty cards bore other patients' names). (6) Record of drugs/injections administered (Narinder Singh). (7) Qualification matching the procedure (Devikar and Prasenjit Das: BDS practising orthodontics was itself negligence). Fora decided most of these without expert evidence, shifting the burden to the dentist once records were missing (Nizam Institute; res ipsa loquitur in Amas Babbar v. Star Dental Centre, Delhi DC 25 Oct 2025, Rs 6.17 lakh for a retained endodontic needle in a 5-year-old).

**Why it matters:** Clinical-record module mandatory fields (defensibility, not regulation): per-tooth diagnosis with linked pre-op radiograph (block 'start RCT' without an attached IOPA/OPG or a documented reason), structured treatment plan with versioning, per-visit progress note, drugs/anaesthetic administered (name, dose, batch), materials/lab work with patient-bound lab slip and warranty card, itemised estimate -> invoice -> receipt chain with variance notes, and the treating dentist's qualification/speciality on each procedure. Make 'print the complete file in 72 hours' a one-click export: the cases show a complete, consistent file is the dentist's best defence (Jose Michael), and an inconsistent one is what converts a bad outcome into liability.

### 11. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

Limitation and retention sources conflict and must be reconciled: CPA 2019 s.69(1): fora 'shall not admit a complaint unless it is filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action has arisen', with s.69(2) condonation for 'sufficient cause' (reasons recorded) - fora routinely treat the date of knowledge of the defect as the trigger. Limitation Act 1963 s.6(1): a minor 'may institute the suit or make the application within the same period after the disability has ceased', so a child patient's clock starts at 18. DCI Code 3.3.1: three years from commencement of treatment. Income-tax Rule 6F (s.44AA): persons carrying on the 'medical' profession must keep, in addition to cash book/ledger, 'a daily case register in Form No. 3C' (patient name, service, fees, date) and an inventory of medicines, retained six years from the end of the relevant assessment year; the rule names 'medical', not 'dental', but ITAT decisions apply it to any medical practice and dentists are treated as within it in practice. EHR Standards 2016: 'all records must compulsorily be preserved and not destroyed during the life-time of the person, ever'; after death with no pending case, 'it is preferable to follow the three (3) year rule where all records of a deceased are made inactive three (3) years after death', and providers are 'strongly encouraged' never to destroy records. DPDP Act 2023 s.8(7): a data fiduciary must erase personal data when consent is withdrawn or 'as soon as it is reasonable to assume that the specified purpose is no longer being served... unless retention is necessary for compliance with any law for the time being in force'. Observed litigation latency: treatment 2018 -> SCDRC decision 2026 (Sabka Dentist, Dewan), 2008 -> HC 2024 (Sravani), 2014 -> 2024 (Prasenjit Das).

**Why it matters:** Retention policy (recommended default, configurable per clinic/country): adults - full clinical record, radiographs, consent, estimates/invoices/receipts and prescriptions kept 10 years from the last visit (covers CPA 2y + condonation, civil 3y, Rule 6F 6y, and the 7-8 year appeal tails seen); minors - until the patient turns 28 or 10 years from last visit, whichever is later; implant/prosthesis cases - lifetime of the device (flag for 15-year retention); deceased patients - 3 years then 'inactive'. Never hard-delete clinical records automatically; implement 'inactive/archived' state, legal-hold flag, and a DPDP erasure request path that erases marketing/CRM data immediately but refuses clinical-record erasure while a legal-retention basis applies (log the basis). Record 'first consult' and 'last visit' per patient as the retention anchors, and keep the Form 3C-style daily case register exportable by financial year.

### 14. `gap-fill-unverified` · confidence high

Dentists' liability under the Consumer Protection Act 2019 is undisputed in the cases reviewed (all nine 2020-2026 dental matters proceeded on the merits before District/State/National Commissions, with awards of Rs 30,000 to ~Rs 9.6 lakh, interest 6-12%, and 'jointly and severally' liability imposed on clinic brand owners and the treating dentist: Sabka Dentist, Amas Babbar/Clove Dental, Narinder Singh). The negligence test is Bolam as adopted in Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005) 6 SCC 1 ('The test for determining medical negligence as laid down in Bolam's case... holds good in its applicability in India'), but fora bypass expert evidence where the failure is documentary (Devikar, Prasenjit Das, Gaganpreet Kaur) or where the injury 'speaks for itself' (Amas Babbar). Vijayalakshmi v. Saraswathi (TN SCDRC 2023) adds that a negligence cause of action does not survive against a deceased dentist's heirs unless already decreed.

**Why it matters:** Multi-chair chains and franchisors using the app are exposed for their associates' documentation, so clinic-level compliance dashboards (missing radiograph before RCT, unsigned consent, estimate/invoice variance, prescription without registration number) are a sellable owner feature, not just a dentist one. Professional-indemnity insurers (OP3 in Narinder Singh) are a distribution channel: an audit-ready record export is what they ask for.
